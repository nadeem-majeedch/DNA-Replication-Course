DNA Replication — Multiple Choice Questions



40 Questions with Answers and Explanations



Questions 1-10: Fundamentals



1. The Meselson-Stahl experiment demonstrated that DNA replication is:
  • A) Conservative

  • B) Semiconservative

  • C) Dispersive

  • D) Discontinuous


    • ✅ Answer: B — After one generation in ¹⁴N, all DNA was hybrid density (one ¹⁵N strand, one ¹⁴N strand), confirming semiconservative replication.



    2. DNA polymerase can synthesize DNA in which direction?
  • A) 3'→5' only

  • B) 5'→3' only

  • C) Both 3'→5' and 5'→3'

  • D) Direction depends on the template


    • ✅ Answer: B — DNA polymerase catalyzes nucleophilic attack by 3'-OH on the α-phosphate of incoming dNTP. This reaction only works in the 5'→3' direction. No known exception exists.



    3. Why does DNA replication require a primer?
  • A) To provide energy for the reaction

  • B) To stabilize the DNA helix

  • C) To provide a free 3'-OH group for DNA polymerase

  • D) To recruit helicase to the origin


    • ✅ Answer: C — DNA polymerase cannot initiate synthesis de novo. It requires a pre-existing 3'-OH group to extend from. Primase synthesizes RNA primers to provide this.



    4. In the lagging strand, Okazaki fragments are oriented:
  • A) 3'→5' relative to fork movement

  • B) 5'→3' away from the fork

  • C) 5'→3' toward the fork

  • D) Randomly


    • ✅ Answer: B — Each Okazaki fragment is synthesized 5'→3' (always), but the overall direction of lagging strand synthesis is away from the replication fork.



    5. The sliding clamp in E. coli is:
  • A) A monomer that binds DNA

  • B) A homodimer forming a ring

  • C) A homotrimer forming a ring

  • D) A hexamer that unwinds DNA


    • ✅ Answer: B — The β sliding clamp is a homodimer (β₂) forming a toroidal ring. The eukaryotic equivalent (PCNA) is a homotrimer.



    6. Which E. coli polymerase has 5'→3' exonuclease activity?
  • A) Pol I

  • B) Pol II

  • C) Pol III

  • D) All of them


    • ✅ Answer: A — Pol I is unique among E. coli polymerases in having 5'→3' exonuclease activity, which is essential for removing RNA primers.



    7. DNA gyrase is unique because it:
  • A) Can only relax positive supercoils

  • B) Can introduce negative supercoils using ATP

  • C) Is a type I topoisomerase

  • D) Is found in both bacteria and humans


    • ✅ Answer: B — DNA gyrase is the only topoisomerase that can introduce negative supercoils. It uses ATP and creates a double-strand break to pass another segment through.



    8. The end-replication problem affects:
  • A) Both linear and circular chromosomes equally

  • B) Only circular chromosomes

  • C) Only linear chromosomes

  • D) Prokaryotic chromosomes only


    • ✅ Answer: C — Linear chromosomes cannot fully replicate at their 5' ends because RNA primers at the lagging strand terminus are removed but cannot be replaced. Circular chromosomes don't have ends.



    9. In E. coli, the origin of replication is called:
  • A) ARS

  • B) oriC

  • C) ORC

  • D) TER


    • ✅ Answer: B — oriC is the single origin in E. coli. ARS (Autonomously Replicating Sequence) is used in yeast; ORC is the origin recognition complex in eukaryotes.



    10. Semiconservative replication was proven by:
  • A) Watson and Crick

  • B) Kornberg

  • C) Meselson and Stahl

  • D) Okazaki


    • ✅ Answer: C — Meselson and Stahl (1958) used ¹⁵N/¹⁴N density gradient centrifugation to demonstrate semiconservative replication.



    Questions 11-20: Prokaryotic Replication



    11. DnaA protein functions in:
  • A) Unwinding DNA at the replication fork

  • B) Synthesizing RNA primers

  • C) Initiating replication at oriC

  • D) Joining Okazaki fragments


    • ✅ Answer: C — DnaA-ATP binds DnaA boxes at oriC and induces melting of the AT-rich region, initiating replication.



    12. DnaB helicase moves in which direction on DNA?
  • A) 3'→5'

  • B) 5'→3'

  • C) Bidirectionally

  • D) Direction depends on strand


    • ✅ Answer: B — DnaB is a 5'→3' helicase. It encircles the lagging strand template and translocates 5'→3' to unwind DNA.



    13. The function of DnaC is to:
  • A) Synthesize RNA primers

  • B) Load DnaB helicase onto DNA

  • C) Remove RNA primers

  • D) Seal nicks between Okazaki fragments


    • ✅ Answer: B — DnaC is a helicase loader that delivers DnaB to single-stranded DNA at the origin. It holds DnaB inactive during loading, then dissociates.



    14. The τ (tau) subunit of Pol III is important for:
  • A) Proofreading

  • B) Dimerizing two Pol III cores (one per strand)

  • C) Loading the β clamp

  • D) Removing RNA primers


    • ✅ Answer: B — τ subunit dimerizes two Pol III core complexes and connects them to DnaB helicase, coordinating leading and lagging strand synthesis.



    15. What is the size of Okazaki fragments in E. coli?
  • A) 100-200 nt

  • B) 500-700 nt

  • C) 1,000-2,000 nt

  • D) 5,000-10,000 nt


    • ✅ Answer: C — E. coli Okazaki fragments are 1,000-2,000 nt. In eukaryotes, they are 100-200 nt.



    16. In E. coli mismatch repair, strand discrimination is based on:
  • A) Strand breaks at Okazaki fragment junctions

  • B) Dam methylation of GATC sequences

  • C) PCNA orientation

  • D) Histone modification


    • ✅ Answer: B — E. coli uses Dam methylation. The parental strand is methylated; the new strand is temporarily unmethylated (hemimethylated). MutH nicks the unmethylated strand.



    17. The Tus/Ter system in E. coli functions to:
  • A) Initiate replication

  • B) Increase fork speed

  • C) Trap replication forks at termination

  • D) Remove RNA primers


    • ✅ Answer: C — Tus binds Ter sequences and creates a polar fork barrier, trapping converging forks in the termination region.



    18. DNA ligase in E. coli uses which cofactor?
  • A) ATP

  • B) GTP

  • C) NAD⁺

  • D) FAD


    • ✅ Answer: C — E. coli DNA ligase uses NAD⁺. Eukaryotic DNA ligases use ATP. This is a key biochemical difference.



    19. The "trombone model" describes:
  • A) How helicase moves along DNA

  • B) How the lagging strand template loops to coordinate synthesis with the leading strand

  • C) How primase interacts with DnaB

  • D) How topoisomerase relieves supercoiling


    • ✅ Answer: B — The trombone model describes how the lagging strand template loops back so that both Pol III cores at the fork can physically move in the same direction.



    20. DNA gyrase is inhibited by:
  • A) Rifampicin

  • B) Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin)

  • C) Ampicillin

  • D) Tetracycline


    • ✅ Answer: B — Fluoroquinolones trap the gyrase-DNA covalent complex, causing lethal double-strand breaks. Rifampicin targets RNA polymerase.



    Questions 21-30: Eukaryotic Replication



    21. The eukaryotic sliding clamp is:
  • A) β₂ homodimer

  • B) PCNA homotrimer

  • C) RFC pentamer

  • D) MCM2-7 hexamer


    • ✅ Answer: B — PCNA (Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen) is a homotrimer forming a ring, similar in function to the bacterial β₂ dimer.



    22. Which eukaryotic polymerase synthesizes the leading strand?
  • A) Pol α

  • B) Pol δ

  • C) Pol ε

  • D) Pol γ


    • ✅ Answer: C — Pol ε is the primary leading strand polymerase. Pol δ synthesizes the lagging strand. Pol α/primase initiates synthesis.



    23. What is the function of geminin?
  • A) Activates origins in S phase

  • B) Inhibits Cdt1 to prevent re-replication

  • C) Loads MCM helicase

  • D) Phosphorylates CDK substrates


    • ✅ Answer: B — Geminin binds and inhibits Cdt1 in S/G2 phase, preventing new MCM loading and thus preventing re-replication.



    24. The CMG helicase consists of:
  • A) DnaB + DnaC + SSB

  • B) Cdc45 + MCM2-7 + GINS

  • C) ORC + Cdc6 + Cdt1

  • D) Pol ε + PCNA + RFC


    • ✅ Answer: B — CMG = Cdc45-MCM-GINS. It is the active replicative helicase in eukaryotes, formed when Cdc45 and GINS bind phosphorylated MCM2-7.



    25. PCNA is loaded onto DNA by:
  • A) Pol α/primase

  • B) RPA

  • C) RFC (Replication Factor C)

  • D) MCM2-7


    • ✅ Answer: C — RFC (clamp loader) uses ATP hydrolysis to open the PCNA ring and load it onto primer-template junctions.



    26. RNase H2 in eukaryotes functions to:
  • A) Remove DNA mismatches

  • B) Remove RNA primers during Okazaki fragment processing

  • C) Unwind DNA at the replication fork

  • D) Synthesize RNA primers


    • ✅ Answer: B — RNase H2 degrades most of the RNA primer, leaving 1-2 ribonucleotides that are then removed by FEN1.



    27. Replication licensing occurs in which cell cycle phase?
  • A) S phase

  • B) G2 phase

  • C) M phase

  • D) G1 phase


    • ✅ Answer: D — Licensing occurs in G1 when CDK activity is low. Pre-RC assembly (ORC → Cdc6 → Cdt1 → MCM) can only occur when CDK is inactive.



    28. How many origins of replication does a human cell have approximately?
  • A) 1

  • B) 100

  • C) 30,000-50,000

  • D) 1,000,000


    • ✅ Answer: C — Human cells have approximately 30,000-50,000 origins. This allows the large genome to be replicated in ~6-8 hours.



    29. The size of Okazaki fragments in human cells is approximately:
  • A) 1,000-2,000 nt

  • B) 500-1,000 nt

  • C) 100-200 nt

  • D) 50-100 nt


    • ✅ Answer: C — Human Okazaki fragments are 100-200 nt, much shorter than E. coli's 1-2 kb fragments (due to nucleosome obstacles).



    30. FEN1 functions in:
  • A) Unwinding DNA

  • B) Removing RNA primers and DNA flaps

  • C) Loading PCNA

  • D) Synthesizing RNA primers


    • ✅ Answer: B — FEN1 (Flap Endonuclease 1) removes 5' flap structures created during Okazaki fragment processing after Pol δ displacement synthesis.



    Questions 31-40: Advanced Topics



    31. Lynch syndrome is caused by mutations in:
  • A) BRCA1/BRCA2

  • B) MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2

  • C) TP53

  • D) APC


    • ✅ Answer: B — Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) is caused by germline mutations in mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2), leading to microsatellite instability.



    32. Telomerase is:
  • A) A DNA-dependent DNA polymerase

  • B) An RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (reverse transcriptase)

  • C) An RNA-dependent RNA polymerase

  • D) A DNA-dependent RNA polymerase


    • ✅ Answer: B — Telomerase (TERT) is a reverse transcriptase that uses its RNA component (TERC) as a template to synthesize telomeric DNA.



    33. The TERT promoter mutations found in cancer:
  • A) Inactivate telomerase

  • B) Create new transcription factor binding sites → increase TERT expression

  • C) Change the telomere repeat sequence

  • D) Delete the entire TERT gene


    • ✅ Answer: B — C228T and C250T mutations create new ETS/GABP binding sites, increasing TERT transcription and telomerase activity.



    34. ALT (Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres) uses:
  • A) Telomerase

  • B) Homologous recombination

  • C) Translesion synthesis

  • D) Non-homologous end joining


    • ✅ Answer: B — ALT uses homologous recombination between telomeric sequences to maintain telomeres without telomerase. It results in highly heterogeneous telomere lengths.



    35. The ATR-CHK1 checkpoint is activated by:
  • A) Double-strand breaks

  • B) Excessive single-stranded DNA at stalled replication forks

  • C) Unattached kinetochores

  • D) Telomere shortening


    • ✅ Answer: B — ATR is activated by RPA-coated ssDNA, which accumulates at stalled replication forks. ATRIP binds RPA-ssDNA and recruits ATR.



    36. Translesion synthesis differs from template switching in that:
  • A) TLS is error-free; template switching is error-prone

  • B) TLS uses specialized polymerases; template switching uses the sister chromatid

  • C) TLS requires PCNA; template switching does not

  • D) TLS only occurs in bacteria


    • ✅ Answer: B — Translesion synthesis uses low-fidelity specialized polymerases (Pol η, ι, κ) to bypass lesions (error-prone). Template switching uses the intact sister chromatid as template (error-free).



    37. Common fragile sites in cancer are:
  • A) Regions where tumor suppressors are located

  • B) Large, gene-rich regions prone to breakage under replication stress

  • C) Regions where telomeres are located

  • D) Origins of replication


    • ✅ Answer: B — Common fragile sites are large genomic regions (>1 Mb genes) that are difficult to replicate. They show gaps/breaks under replication stress and are frequent breakpoints in cancer.



    38. Oncogene-induced replication stress is caused by:
  • A) Telomere shortening

  • B) Excessive origin firing, transcription-replication conflicts, and dNTP imbalance

  • C) Loss of p53

  • D) Telomerase activation


    • ✅ Answer: B — Oncogenes (Ras, Myc, Cyclin E) cause aberrant origin firing, increase transcription, and create dNTP imbalances, all contributing to replication stress.



    39. BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations increase cancer risk because they impair:
  • A) Mismatch repair

  • B) Base excision repair

  • C) Homologous recombination

  • D) Nucleotide excision repair


    • ✅ Answer: C — BRCA1 and BRCA2 are essential for homologous recombination. Loss of HR → genome instability → cancer predisposition (breast, ovarian).



    40. PARP inhibitors exploit synthetic lethality in:
  • A) All cancer cells

  • B) BRCA1/BRCA2-mutant cancer cells

  • C) Telomerase-positive cancer cells

  • D) MSI-High cancer cells


    • ✅ Answer: B — PARP inhibitors prevent SSB repair → SSBs converted to DSBs during replication → in BRCA-deficient cells (no HR), DSBs cannot be repaired → cell death. Normal cells survive because HR is intact.

    DNA Replication — Viva Voce Questions



    30 Questions for Oral Examination



    Basic Concepts (1-8)



    1. What is DNA replication and why is it essential for life?
  • Expected answer: Semiconservative copying of genome before cell division; ensures each daughter cell gets complete genetic information


    • 2. What are the three models of DNA replication? Which was proven correct?
  • Expected answer: Conservative, semiconservative, dispersive. Semiconservative proven by Meselson-Stahl (1958)


    • 3. Can DNA polymerase synthesize DNA in the 3'→5' direction? Why or why not?
  • Expected answer: No. Chemical mechanism requires 3'-OH for nucleophilic attack on incoming dNTP. Universal rule in all organisms.


    • 4. What is the difference between a leading strand and a lagging strand?
  • Expected answer: Both synthesized 5'→3'. Leading: continuous toward fork. Lagging: discontinuous (Okazaki fragments) away from fork.


    • 5. Why do we need RNA primers for DNA replication?
  • Expected answer: DNA polymerase cannot initiate synthesis de novo; needs free 3'-OH. Primase provides this with RNA primer.


    • 6. What is the sliding clamp and why is it important?
  • Expected answer: Ring-shaped protein (β₂ in bacteria, PCNA in eukaryotes) that tethers polymerase to template. Increases processivity from ~20 to >50,000 nt.


    • 7. What is proofreading and how does it improve replication fidelity?
  • Expected answer: 3'→5' exonuclease activity of polymerase removes misincorporated nucleotides. Improves fidelity 100-fold (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷).


    • 8. What is the overall error rate of DNA replication after all correction mechanisms?
  • Expected answer: ~10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹⁰ per nucleotide per cell division. Three layers: selection (10⁻⁵), proofreading (10⁻⁷), MMR (10⁻⁹–10⁻¹⁰)




    • Prokaryotic Replication (9-15)



    9. Describe the structure and function of oriC.
  • Expected answer: 245 bp origin; DnaA boxes (9-mers) for DnaA binding; AT-rich 13-mers for melting. Single origin in E. coli.


    • 10. What is the role of DnaA in replication initiation?
  • Expected answer: Initiator protein. DnaA-ATP binds DnaA boxes cooperatively, induces positive supercoiling, melts AT-rich region.


    • 11. Explain how DnaB helicase is loaded onto DNA.
  • Expected answer: DnaC holds DnaB inactive → delivers to melted origin → DnaB encircles ssDNA → DnaC dissociates (ATP-dependent)


    • 12. What is unique about DNA Pol I compared to other E. coli polymerases?
  • Expected answer: Has 5'→3' exonuclease activity for RNA primer removal. Also has polymerase and 3'→5' exonuclease (proofreading).


    • 13. Why does DNA ligase in E. coli use NAD⁺ instead of ATP?
  • Expected answer: Biochemical difference from eukaryotes. Same mechanism (adenylation) but different cofactor. Eukaryotic/viral ligases use ATP.


    • 14. How does the Tus/Ter system ensure termination occurs in the correct region?
  • Expected answer: Tus binds Ter sequences → polar fork barrier → blocks fork in one direction but not other → traps both forks in termination region.


    • 15. What would happen if DNA gyrase were inactivated in E. coli?
  • Expected answer: Positive supercoils accumulate ahead of fork → fork stalling → DNA breakage → cell death. Gyrase is essential.




    • Eukaryotic Replication (16-22)



    16. What is replication licensing and when does it occur?
  • Expected answer: Assembly of pre-RC (ORC→Cdc6→Cdt1→MCM) in G1 when CDK low. Ensures each origin fires once per cell cycle.


    • 17. Why do eukaryotes need multiple origins of replication?
  • Expected answer: Large genome (3.2 × 10⁹ bp); single origin would take weeks. Multiple origins (~30,000-50,000) allow replication in 6-8 hours.


    • 18. What is the CMG helicase and how is it activated?
  • Expected answer: Cdc45-MCM2-7-GINS. Inactive MCM loaded at origins → DDK + CDK phosphorylate → Cdc45 and GINS bind → active helicase.


    • 19. Name the three major eukaryotic replicative polymerases and their functions.
  • Expected answer: Pol α/primase (initiates), Pol ε (leading strand), Pol δ (lagging strand + repair)


    • 20. What is the role of PCNA in mismatch repair?
  • Expected answer: RFC loads PCNA in oriented manner; MutLα recognizes PCNA orientation → nicks new strand → directs repair to correct strand.


    • 21. How are Okazaki fragments processed in eukaryotes?
  • Expected answer: RNase H2 removes most RNA → FEN1 removes remaining RNA + flaps → Pol δ fills gaps → DNA Ligase I seals nicks


    • 22. What is the difference between early and late replication in S phase?
  • Expected answer: Euchromatin (active genes) replicates early; heterochromatin replicates late. Correlates with gene expression and epigenetic state.




    • Telomeres and Checkpoints (23-27)



    23. Explain the end-replication problem in one sentence.
  • Expected answer: Linear chromosomes cannot fully replicate at 5' ends because RNA primers at the terminus are removed but cannot be replaced.


    • 24. What are the two essential components of telomerase?
  • Expected answer: TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase, protein) and TERC (telomerase RNA component, contains template)


    • 25. Why do most somatic cells not express telomerase?
  • Expected answer: Telomere shortening acts as a biological clock limiting cell divisions (tumor suppression). Stem cells and germ cells maintain telomerase.


    • 26. How is the ATR-CHK1 checkpoint activated?
  • Expected answer: Stalled fork → ssDNA accumulates → RPA binds → ATRIP recruits ATR → ATR phosphorylates CHK1 → fork stabilization, origin suppression


    • 27. What is the clinical significance of the TERT C228T promoter mutation?
  • Expected answer: Creates ETS binding site → increased TERT transcription → telomerase reactivation → immortalization → found in many cancers




    • Applications and Disease (28-30)



    28. How do fluoroquinolone antibiotics target bacterial replication?
  • Expected answer: Trap gyrase-DNA complex → stabilize DSBs → fork collapse → cell death. Selective for bacterial gyrase over human topoisomerases.


    • 29. Explain how PARP inhibitors work in BRCA-mutant cancers.
  • Expected answer: Synthetic lethality. BRCA-mutant cells lack HR; PARP inhibition prevents SSB repair → SSBs become DSBs → cannot repair → cell death.


    • 30. A patient has microsatellite instability in their colorectal cancer. What gene mutations would you suspect?
  • Expected answer: Mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2). Likely Lynch syndrome (HNPCC). Recommend genetic testing and family screening.




    • Bonus Questions (31-33)



    31. What would happen if all DnaA in E. coli were replaced with a mutant that cannot hydrolyze ATP?
  • Expected answer: DnaA-ATP would accumulate → continuous origin firing → re-replication → gene amplification → genome instability → likely lethal


    • 32. How does the cell prevent the lagging strand Pol III from continuously falling off the template?
  • Expected answer: β sliding clamp increases processivity; trombone model coordinates with leading strand; τ subunit links both polymerases


    • 33. A research lab discovers a protein that binds specifically to replication forks and promotes fork reversal. What would be the phenotype if this protein were knocked out?
  • Expected answer: Reduced fork reversal → increased fork collapse → more DSBs → genome instability → sensitivity to replication stress

    Case 1


  • Lynch syndrome — germline MLH1 mutation (pathogenic frameshift)

  • Methylation negative = hereditary (not sporadic); sporadic cases show MLH1 promoter methylation

  • 50% risk for children (autosomal dominant)

  • Annual colonoscopy from age 20-25; endometrial screening

  • Anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab) — MSI-H = high neoantigen load


    • Case 2


  • Dyskeratosis congenita (TERT mutation)

  • Gly761Glu affects TERT catalytic activity → reduced telomere extension

  • Bone marrow has high cell turnover → most sensitive to telomere shortening

  • BMT may help but conditioning regimen must be reduced (treosulfan-based)

  • Links to normal aging — telomere shortening is part of aging process


    • Case 3


  • Ligase mutant — gaps between Okazaki fragments (normal fragments but not joined)

  • Okazaki fragments synthesized normally but ligase cannot seal nicks

  • Gaps in DNA → chromosome breaks during next replication → genome instability

  • Complementation test — introduce wild-type ligase gene on plasmid

  • Should rescue if plasmid expresses functional ligase


    • Case 4


  • PARP inhibitor — causes DSBs during replication

  • BRCA1-mutant cells already have HR defect → PARP inhibition adds less toxicity (but actually more complex — BRCA1-WT cells show more sensitivity because Drug X specifically targets fork restart pathway)

  • Not as monotherapy — resistance develops; use in combination

  • Combine with platinum chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitor

  • γH2AX foci, BRCA1/2 status as biomarkers


    • Case 5


  • ALT (Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres)

  • APBs (ALT-associated PML bodies), C-circles, heterogeneous telomere length

  • Homologous recombination between telomeric sequences (vs. reverse transcriptase)

  • No — telomerase inhibitors won't work on telomerase-negative cells

  • Target recombination (ATR inhibitors, PARP inhibitors)


    • Case 6


  • Fork slowing — CldU tracks shortened → drug inhibits fork progression

  • Rad51 needed for fork restart → without it, stalled forks collapse

  • Fork reversal is protective → prevents collapse; loss of reversal = collapse

  • Genetic screen — identify genes that rescue Drug Y sensitivity

  • More sensitive — BRCA-deficient cells already have fork restart defects


    • Case 7


  • Patient 1: FEN1 or RNase H (primer processing defect)

  • Patient 2: DNA Ligase I (ligation defect)

  • Patient 3: MSH2/MLH1 (mismatch repair defect)

  • Patient 1: Potential cancer predisposition (FEN1 mutations linked to cancer)

  • Patient 2: LIG1 syndrome-like phenotype

  • Patient 3: Lynch syndrome-like phenotype


    • Case 8


  • Adjacent origins compensate — origins can fire from nearby regions

  • Higher temperature = slower fork progression → origin deletion becomes critical

  • Longer distance between origins → fork stalling at difficult-to-replicate regions

  • Origin usage correlates with genome stability — loss increases fragility

  • Common fragile sites may correspond to regions with few origins


    • Case 9


  • Pol ε essential for leading strand — no compensation possible

  • Physical interaction coordinates polymerase switching at the fork

  • Remaining polymerase can partially compensate (with reduced efficiency)

  • Likely lethal — loss of both leading and lagging strand polymerases

  • Confirms Pol ε = leading, Pol δ = lagging assignments


    • Case 10


  • MLH1 loss → defective MMR → cannot repair replication errors → SNV accumulation

  • LOH (loss of heterozygosity) — second allele deleted → complete MMR loss

  • TERT promoter mutation → telomerase reactivation → telomere maintenance

  • Aneuploidy → chromosome instability from defective checkpoints

  • Immunotherapy (anti-PD-1 for MSI-H) + PARP inhibitor if HR-deficient
